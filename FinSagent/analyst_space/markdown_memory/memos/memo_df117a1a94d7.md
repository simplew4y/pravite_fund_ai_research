## 竞争格局

NVIDIA continues to dominate the global AI accelerator market, reporting $44.1 billion in Q1 FY2026 revenue—a 69% year-over-year increase—driven primarily by Data Center sales of $39.1 billion [4]. However, its competitive position faces mounting pressure due to geopolitical constraints, particularly in China. On April 9, 2025, the U.S. government imposed new export controls requiring licenses for NVIDIA’s H20 chips and similar products destined for China and D:5 countries, effectively foreclosing NVIDIA from China’s data center market [1]. This restriction triggered a $4.5 billion charge related to excess H20 inventory and purchase obligations, reflecting sharply diminished demand [1]. The inability to field a compliant, competitive product approved by both U.S. and Chinese authorities has ceded strategic ground to rivals, enabling them to expand developer ecosystems and customer bases that could challenge NVIDIA globally [1].

Key competitors are capitalizing on this vacuum. While NVIDIA’s peer group includes companies like Meta Platforms—added due to comparable scale and talent competition—its true hardware rivals include AMD, Intel, and domestic Chinese firms such as Huawei and Biren [3]. Huawei’s Ascend 910B, for instance, is gaining traction in China’s AI infrastructure as a sanctioned alternative to NVIDIA’s A/H-series GPUs. With NVIDIA absent from this critical growth market, competitors can deepen relationships with local cloud providers and hyperscalers, fostering software stack loyalty (e.g., CUDA alternatives) that may erode NVIDIA’s long-standing ecosystem advantage [1]. Notably, NVIDIA’s market cap of $1.07 trillion far exceeds even the 75th percentile of its peer group ($329.7B), underscoring its premium valuation but also highlighting vulnerability if market share shifts [9].

NVIDIA’s core competitive advantages remain its full-stack AI platform—including CUDA, libraries, and enterprise support—and manufacturing leadership via TSMC’s cutting-edge nodes. Yet, these strengths are less relevant where geopolitics block access. Conversely, competitors like AMD benefit from slightly less restrictive U.S. export rules, allowing limited sales of MI300X chips to China, while Chinese firms operate without U.S. constraints domestically [1]. Industry trends intensifying competition include the global push for AI sovereignty, accelerated adoption of open-source AI frameworks (reducing CUDA lock-in), and vertical integration by hyperscalers developing in-house accelerators (e.g., Google TPUs, Amazon Trainium).
